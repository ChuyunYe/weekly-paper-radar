    Nonlinear Sufficient Dimension Reduction for Causal Inference with Continuous Treatments

    Chak Fung Choi, Yin Tang, Bing Li · 2026-10-29 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Chak Fung Choi
    Yin Tang
    Bing Li

    Keywords: stat.ME

    Abstract

    Causal inference with continuous treatments requires estimating nuisance functions to adjust for the potentially high-dimensional confounder, but nonparametric estimation of these functions is severely hampered by the curse of dimensionality. To address this challenge, we propose a nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction framework to construct low-dimensional sufficient confounders to preserve the unbiasedness of the treatment effect. We estimate the causal effect by combining the generalized Sliced Inverse Regression with the inverse probability weighting, outcome regression, and augmented inverse probability weighting, respectively. We further establish the convergence rates of the first two estimators and derive the asymptotic distributions of the third. Our theoretical results show that, compared to estimators based on the full covariates, dimension reduction yields faster convergence rates and weaker rate conditions without inflating the asymptotic variance. Simulation studies demonstrate that the proposed estimators improve finite-sample performance over their counterparts without dimension reduction, particularly when the original covariate dimension is high. These results demonstrate that sufficient dimension reduction provides a flexible approach to mitigating the curse of dimensionality in causal inference with continuous treatments.

    Not Just Why, but When and for Whom: Revisiting Common Estimands in Causal Inference

    Lucius E. J. Bynum, George Perrett, Marc A. Scott, Jennifer L. Hill · 2026-10-26 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Lucius E. J. Bynum
    George Perrett
    Marc A. Scott
    Jennifer L. Hill

    Keywords: stat.ME

    Abstract

    Causal inference commonly defines an individual treatment effect as a quantity that varies across units. But we know such effects, even for a fixed unit, can also vary over time, across repeated studies, or over different methods of measurement, and they can even exhibit inherent stochasticity. In this work, we consider how such additional sources of variation create a need for more general notions of average treatment effects, including some estimands that have yet to be more formally explored by the causal inference community. This perspective also motivates a reconceptualization of our most common existing estimands, such as conditional average, sample average, and individual treatment effects (CATEs, SATEs, and ITEs), as subject to more axes of variation than are typically considered. We explore two main consequences of this reframing in the case where we have data from a single randomized controlled trial: (1) decision making in fixed samples and (2) uncertainty quantification during estimation. We find that different estimands, even for the same unit or the same sample, can qualitatively disagree on whether an effect is positive or negative. We demonstrate these issues empirically through a case study with real data and characterize through a simulation study how our assumptions about these sources of variation, even if we cannot observe the variation, can be consequential for estimation and proper coverage performance. Importantly, these phenomena arise even in the simplest of cases, where all structural and parametric assumptions are satisfied. More broadly, this paper aims to provide a preliminary investigation into an underexplored class of assumptions --- assumptions about unobserved non-confounders --- that should be considered when estimating treatment effects.

    Measuring Activity-Space Segregation with Multi-Source Big Data: A Physical--Digital Framework with Simulation Validation and a Real-Data Proof of Concept

    Guangwei Wang · 2026-12-31 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Guangwei Wang

    Keywords: co-presence, digital media exposure, digital interaction, Foursquare check-ins, socioeconomic segregation, physical mobility, place semantics, multi-source big data, simulation validation, measurement error

    Journal: PhilPapers (PhilPapers Foundation)

    Abstract

    Segregation research increasingly measures who people encounter beyond home, yet physical mobility, place semantics, and digital activity are often combined without clear construct boundaries. This study develops a physical--digital activity-space framework that distinguishes co-presence, digital exposure, and digital interaction, and represents physical and digital segregation as separate components rather than a forced composite. Validation is deliberately split by evidentiary role. A public Washington--Baltimore Foursquare check-in subset tests the computational feasibility and robustness of a discrete group co-visitation statistic; it does not validate continuous socioeconomic exposure because the dataset lacks individual socioeconomic status. Monte Carlo experiments separately test layer-selective construct response and sensitivity to trace missingness, spatial aggregation, socioeconomic measurement error, and cross-layer coupling. The results show that the proposed calculations are executable and that the synthetic physical and digital components respond selectively to their generating mechanisms, while also revealing important measurement attenuation. No real-data estimate of income segregation, digital exposure, physical--digital coupling, or the causal effect of inequality is reported. The framework therefore combines bounded validation with a falsifiable comparative and causal research agenda.

    Functional-Specific Information Borrowing under Limited Overlap

    Shinichiro Shirota · 2026-10-30 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Shinichiro Shirota

    Keywords: stat.ME

    Abstract

    Source observations can improve a target average without estimating the entire shared response accurately. We study this distinction in two independent observational samples whose treatment effects share a rough component and differ by a smoother discrepancy. For power-law treatment overlap known for the analysis, known design density, fixed positive Sobolev radii, and Gaussian noise, we derive matching minimax mean squared error bounds with three regimes. Two explicit source-information thresholds determine the onset of rate improvement and attainment of the source-known rate. A signed local correction explains the thresholds: source observations estimate only the part of the integration functional poorly recovered from the target sample. Direct likelihood lower bounds apply to all estimators. Existing joint reproducing-kernel recovery, which does not require propensity inputs, attains these rates, and standard bias-aware intervals are conditionally honest under the specified class. A supplementary zero-overlap result separates estimation error from residual identification uncertainty. Numerical results illustrate both gains and losses when the total number of participants is fixed. The intermediate rate depends on overlap geometry, while smoothness determines its two endpoints.

    Uncertainty Quantification for Topological Treatment Effects

    Hugo G. Souto, Ioannis Diamantis · 2026-10-30 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Hugo G. Souto
    Ioannis Diamantis

    Keywords: stat.ME, math.ST

    Abstract

    We present a unified framework for uncertainty quantification over filtration scale, extending the topological average treatment effect (TATE) analysis of \citet{kim-lee} to conditional mean confidence and individual effect prediction. For the conditional topological average treatment effect (CTATE), we derive multiplier confidence bands at a fixed covariate value, simultaneous over filtration scale, and prove validity in a correctly specified finite-dimensional regression regime, with explicit control of smoothing bias and nuisance-estimation error. For the individual topological treatment effect (ITTE), we integrate existing weighted and functional split-conformal methods to obtain finite-sample marginal prediction coverage under known target-to-calibration likelihood ratios, with a total-variation bound for estimated ratios. We also extend the current TATE silhouette stability analysis to the diagram-independent bound $\|Φ_r(D)-Φ_r(D')\|_\infty \le (1+2r)W_1(D,D')$ for $r\geq1$, recovering the known $r=1$ constant, and propagate diagram approximation error to prediction bands. The installable Python library \texttt{tcda\_uq} integrates mean confidence, individual prediction, and topological preprocessing, with tutorials and reproduction materials. Simulations and a PACCE lesion-measurement application illustrate the distinct coverage targets and the importance of declaring the target population.

    When Everyone Can Build a Model: Mechanistic Inference in the Age of AI

    Marvin van Aalst, Adèle H. Ribeiro, Anna Matuszyńska · 2026-10-30 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Marvin van Aalst — Computational Life Science, Faculty of Mathematics and Natural Sciences, RWTH Aachen University, Germany
    Adèle H. Ribeiro — Causal and Explainable Artificial Intelligence in Life Sciences, Faculty of Mathematics and Natural Sciences, RWTH Aachen University, Germany; Center for Computational Life Sciences, RWTH Aachen University, Germany
    Anna Matuszyńska — Computational Life Science, Faculty of Mathematics and Natural Sciences, RWTH Aachen University, Germany; Center for Computational Life Sciences, RWTH Aachen University, Germany

    Keywords: cs.LG

    Abstract

    Modern deep learning and AI-assisted code generation are lowering the technical barrier to building mathematical models of biological systems that reproduce complex observations. This lets researchers with domain expertise build semi-mechanistic, hybrid models constrained by prior knowledge and augmented with learned structure without extensive training in modelling. However, easier construction does not make interpretation easier: predictive success of such so-called grey-box models does not establish that an inferred mechanism corresponds to the one that generated the data, and the learned component can absorb errors in the mechanistic part. Many biological modelling problems are underdetermined, so several, possibly infinitely many, models may reproduce the same data equally well. This ambiguity enters at two modelling choices: which variables can affect others, and which response function describes the underlying mechanism. For the first choice, causal inference provides a formal framework stating which causal relationships and intervention effects are identifiable from the data and available knowledge. For the second, ensemble analyses quantify the uncertainty and the range of behaviours still allowed by models that fit equally well. Together, they turn a single good fit into an explicit account of what is supported, what remains open, and which experiment would decide.

    Reinforcement Learning Under Runtime Confounding: Policy Learning and Off-Policy Evaluation

    Raphael Kim, Michele Santacatterina, Ramin Zabih, Keith Barnatchez · 2026-10-29 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Raphael Kim
    Michele Santacatterina
    Ramin Zabih
    Keith Barnatchez

    Keywords: stat.ML, cs.LG, stat.ME

    Abstract

    Reinforcement learning (RL) is increasingly used to train robust, adaptable systems across healthcare, e-commerce, and robotics. In these settings, legal, ethical, or logistical concerns may preclude certain key variables that drive decisions from being collected during deployment time. This gives rise to a setting known as runtime confounding: the logged data hold full observability, but deployment time lacks knowledge of certain variables, known as runtime confounders. Unlike single timepoint and non-adaptive settings, runtime confounding under reinforcement learning has the potential to propagate over time, as earlier decisions affect future states, observations, and action assignments, leading to biased learning. We address these problems with three contributions: we (1) develop policy learning algorithms under runtime-confounding, (2) develop semi-parametric efficient off-policy estimation procedures, and (3) provide diagnostics for understanding the price of runtime confounding.

    Model Selection for Off-Policy Evaluation via Pairwise Bellman Validation

    Lars van der Laan, Vasilis Syrgkanis, Nathan Kallus · 2026-10-26 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Lars van der Laan
    Vasilis Syrgkanis
    Nathan Kallus

    Keywords: stat.ML, cs.LG, econ.EM

    Abstract

    Model selection and hyperparameter tuning pose a central challenge in off-policy evaluation (OPE) because errors of fitted Q-functions and occupancy-ratio estimates cannot be directly evaluated from held-out transitions. Bellman-residual and minimax validation methods often require auxiliary regressions or optimization over separate critic classes, adding computational and tuning costs. We introduce pairwise Bellman validation (PBV), a tuning-free minimax procedure for selecting among finite classes of fitted Q-functions and occupancy-ratio estimators using held-out transitions. PBV constructs critics from pairwise candidate differences, requiring only finitely many empirical moment calculations without auxiliary validation models or separate critic optimization. We establish finite-sample oracle inequalities relative to the best available candidate, even under candidate-class misspecification. Our key insight is that Q-function errors and log ratios between candidate and true occupancy ratios provide oracle critics whose Bellman and adjoint Bellman moments control estimation error. Pairwise candidate differences approximate these unknown critics, while penalties account for comparator approximation error. For Q-function selection, we first develop unweighted PBV, which evaluates pairwise Bellman moments under the offline distribution and admits oracle guarantees under a pairwise coercivity condition relating the offline distribution to the target-policy dynamics. We then develop target-aligned PBV (TA-PBV), which uses marginalized importance weighting to provide error control without pairwise coercivity. For occupancy-ratio selection, we develop adjoint PBV (A-PBV), which evaluates adjoint Bellman moments against log-ratio critics. On Hopper and Deep OPE, unweighted PBV performs competitively, while target-occupancy weighting can improve performance in settings with controlled overlap.

    Fairness-Constrained Distributionally Robust Causal Policy Optimization for Adaptive Instruction in Higher Education

    Sanjeev Vijay · 2026-10-28 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Sanjeev Vijay — Jagannath University; Jagannath University

    Keywords: adaptive instruction, higher education, distributionally robust optimization, structural causal models, doubly robust estimation, confounding, subgroup fairness, distribution shift, multi-objective Bayesian optimization, Lagrangian relaxation, off-policy evaluation, student diversity

    Journal: Iconic Research and Engineering Journals

    Abstract

    Adaptive instructional policies in higher education often optimize short-term engagement at the expense of durable learning gains, and they frequently neglect heterogeneity across student subpopulations. We propose a fairness-constrained distributionally robust causal policy optimization framework that replaces standard deep reinforcement learning agents with a causally grounded decision-making pipeline. A structural causal model of the educational environment is constructed first, and doubly robust estimation is then applied to observational log data so that confounding bias in the counterfactual evaluation of instructional actions is mitigated. To guard against distribution shifts and adversarial perturbations in student populations, the policy optimization problem is formulated as a distributionally robust objective that maximizes worst-case expected utility over an ambiguity set centered on the empirical distribution. The policy itself is represented by reduced-rank policy trees, which preserve interpretability while remaining computationally tractable, and multi-objective Bayesian optimization is employed to balance average performance, cross-subgroup variance, and explicit disparity constraints. Fairness is enforced through Lagrangian relaxation, while causal effect decomposition separates direct instructional effects from those mediated by motivation or cognitive load. Periodic off-policy evaluation and counterfactual subgroup audits provide closed-loop validation, triggering retraining or constraint tightening when inequities emerge. The main contribution is an integrated methodology that unites causal inference, distributional robustness, and subgroup fairness within a single policy learning loop for higher education. This approach is significant because it offers a principled path toward instructional policies that remain effective, equitable, and auditable throughout their deployment lifecycle.

    Adaptive training load optimization for sprinters via offline reinforcement learning and multi-modal physiological feedback

    Xinhe Hu, Linjun Liu, Yuting Pei · 2026-10-28 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Xinhe Hu — Guangxi Normal University
    Linjun Liu — Lincoln University College; Wuhan Sports University
    Yuting Pei — Saint Petersburg State Electrotechnical University

    Keywords: sprint training, offline reinforcement learning, Markov decision process, block periodization, fatigue, acute:chronic workload ratio, countermovement jump, overtraining syndrome, doubly robust estimation, SHAP, elite sprinters, training prescription

    Journal: Frontiers in Psychology

    Abstract

    Elite sprint training requires daily adjustment of training load to balance performance adaptation and fatigue, yet physiological monitoring does not necessarily translate into a specific next-session prescription. This study formalizes daily sprint training prescription as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process and develops an adaptive load-optimization framework that couples multi-modal physiological feedback with offline reinforcement learning (RL). A 19-dimensional state fusing autonomic, neuromuscular, endocrine, subjective, and load-aggregate signals is encoded by a dual-tower network [a one-dimensional convolutional neural network–Transformer temporal branch and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) static branch] over an action space of 62 admissible training-type/intensity/volume combinations. A composite reward penalizes overreaching while rewarding short-term gain, longitudinal adaptation, and acute-to-chronic workload ratio conformity. The policy is learned by conservative off-policy training on retrospective data, evaluated through the Fitted Q-Evaluation (FQE) and Doubly Robust (DR) estimators, and presented to coaches as the three leading candidate actions, with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values used to explain the model's preferences among the candidate actions. In a 12-week single-center retrospective comparison of 70 national-level sprinters (35 men and 35 women; 100 m personal best (PB) ≤ 11.20 s for men or ≤ 12.50 s for women), grouped by whether their squad had adopted the tool ( n = 35 RL-guided) or continued classical block periodisation ( n = 35 control), the RL-guided cohort demonstrated between-cohort differences of +0.240 s in 100 m [95% confidence interval (CI) +0.128 to +0.352; p < 0.001, d = 1.00], +0.104 s in 30 m ( p < 0.001, d = 1.00) and +2.17 cm in countermovement jump ( p < 0.001, d = 1.01). Zero algorithmically defined sustained multi-indicator fatigue/load events were observed in the RL-guided cohort vs. seven in the control cohort (1.43 per 1,000 athlete-days); these events should not be interpreted as clinically diagnosed overtraining syndrome. Off-policy evaluation yielded exploratory point estimates whose precision remains unknown because uncertainty intervals were not calculated. These observational findings describe associations with an RL-guided, coach-in-the-loop workflow and do not establish a causal effect of the RL policy.

    Solving elliptic partial differential equation based on deep reinforcement learning

    Mengyao Li · 2026-10-31 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Mengyao Li — Luoyang Vocational College of Culture and Tourism

    Keywords: Not provided by source

    Journal: Discover Artificial Intelligence

    Abstract

    Elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs) constitute a fundamental class of governing equations in physics and engineering, underpinning the mathematical descriptions of electrostatic potential distribution, steady-state heat conduction, and linear elasticity. The numerical solution of these equations on fine discretization grids is computationally intensive, as classical iterative solvers require numerous sweeps to achieve convergence. This computational bottleneck critically hinders real-time applications such as online process control, interactive design optimization, and inverse problem reconstruction, where rapid response to changing boundary conditions or source terms is essential. The traditional numerical method cannot meet the real-time application requirements because of its long calculation time when solving on fine grids. In this study, the solution framework of deep Q-network is constructed, and the elliptic PDE solution is modeled as Markov decision processes. The state space includes solution field, residual field and boundary mark on discrete grid. The action space is 11 discrete corrections (− 0.10 to 0.10 with an interval of 0.02). The reward function is weighted sum of PDE residual term (weight 0.6), boundary error term (weight 0.3) and smooth regularization term (weight 0.1). The feature extraction network uses three-layer convolution and two residual blocks to output 512-dimensional vectors. The proposed DRL solver achieves relative L2 errors of 0.28%, 0.39%, and 0.62% on Poisson, Helmholtz, and anisotropic diffusion test cases, respectively, outperforming the finite element method (0.32%, 0.45%, 0.78%) and the multigrid method (0.31%, 0.44%, 0.76%). In the inference phase, the DRL solver completes a single forward pass on a 256 × 2562 grid in 0.15 s, achieving a 9898-fold speedup over the multigrid method’s 14.8 s. The training process converges stably within 20,000 steps, with the residual norm decreasing from an initial value of 12.4 to a final value of 0.16. This method provides a feasible way for solving and controlling real-time physical fields.

    Eduardo C. Garrido-Merchán · 2026-10-30 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Eduardo C. Garrido-Merchán

    Keywords: cs.GT, cs.LG

    Abstract

    Reinforcement learning and game theory describe the same object from two sides. Game theory says what a rational outcome of a strategic interaction is; reinforcement learning says how an agent could reach one through experience. The two literatures nevertheless keep separate vocabularies for identical structures, and the treatments that bridge them present the correspondence as analogy rather than theorem. This monograph supplies the theorems. A game is a Markov decision process in three distinct senses, and every table entry is labelled with the one that applies: a definitional identity, an exact reduction carried by a value- and solution-preserving map, or a structural analogy that fails under identifiable conditions. We take every standard class of game, from normal-form through repeated, stochastic, partially observable, extensive-form with imperfect information, Bayesian, graphical, Stackelberg, potential, coalitional, mean-field and evolutionary games, and identify for each the decision-theoretic model that reinforcement learning already studies under another name. We map each model class onto the training procedures sound for it and record the complexity of the associated solution problems. A final part collects the correspondences that do not hold: the non-stationarity that invalidates the independent-learner reduction, the impossibility results that forbid uncoupled convergence to Nash equilibrium, and the gap between the coarse correlated equilibria that no-regret self-play reaches and the correlated equilibria it is credited with.

    Abstract

    Model-based offline multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) provides a promising approach to multi-agent decision-making under partial observability, where the global state is unavailable from offline data. However, limited dataset coverage can lead to substantial out-of-distribution (OOD) model errors, which may mislead policy optimization and degrade downstream performance. Existing model-based offline MARL methods typically rely on access to global states or mitigate model errors through pessimistic or conservative policy optimization, leaving the reliability and refinement of learned world models less explored.
