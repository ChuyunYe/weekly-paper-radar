    Kimia Kamal, Bilal Farooq · 2026-11-07 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Kimia Kamal — Toronto Metropolitan University

    Keywords: travel behavior, causal inference, discrete choice modelling, unmeasured confounding, causal discovery, counterfactual forecasting, normalizing flows, interpretability, pedestrian crossing behavior, virtual reality

    Journal: Transportation Research Part C Emerging Technologies

    Abstract

    Transportation policy assessment often involves causal questions, yet traditional travel behaviour lacks the ability of distinguishing causal association from non-causal correlations. We present the deep CAusal infeRence mOdel for traveL behavIour aNAlysis (CAROLINA) , a novel framework that addresses both observed and unobserved confounders in discrete choice modelling through causal discovery and the strengths of deep neural networks.     Seven myths about causal inference in ecology: clarifications and common ground

    Laura E. Dee, Paul J. Ferraro, Katherine Siegel, Steve Miller et al. · 2026-11-06 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Laura E. Dee — University of Colorado Boulder; University of Colorado System
    Paul J. Ferraro — Johns Hopkins University
    Katherine Siegel — Cooperative Institute for Research in Environmental Sciences
    Steve Miller — University of Colorado Boulder; University of Colorado System
    Jarrett Byrnes — University of Massachusetts Boston
    Hannah Correia — University of North Dakota
    Tyler L. McIntosh — Cooperative Institute for Research in Environmental Sciences; University of Colorado Boulder; University of Colorado System
    Suchinta Arif — University of New Brunswick; University of Colorado Boulder; University of Colorado System

    Keywords: causal inference, causal assumptions, causal claims, skepticism, prior knowledge

    Abstract

    Identifying and quantifying causal relationships from data is a central goal in ecology and conservation. To achieve this goal, ecologists are increasingly applying frameworks and methods from the broader, multidisciplinary causal inference literature. These applications have generated both enthusiasm and skepticism. Here, we address seven common sources of skepticism that we see as myths. Much of this skepticism treats causal inference as a competing research paradigm. However, it is better understood as a set of ideas and practices that can sharpen ecological reasoning and strengthen empirical evidence. The value of causal inference lies in clarifying the assumptions required to interpret statistical associations causally and in encouraging researchers to state these assumptions explicitly, justifying them using prior knowledge and biological reasoning. In this sense, causal inference does not replace what ecologists already do well. Rather, it helps reveal, refine, and communicate the logic already underlying empirical research with causal aims. Across the seven concerns that we examine, a common message emerges: debates about causal inference should focus less on labels and techniques and more on whether the assumptions behind a causal claim are credible, clearly stated, and fit for purpose when making causal claims in ecology.

    Minimax designs and estimators for causal inference under network interference

    Vishesh Karwa, Edoardo M. Airoldi · 2026-11-05 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Vishesh Karwa
    Edoardo M. Airoldi

    Keywords: math.ST, stat.ME

    Abstract

    We develop a design-based minimax theory for linear causal estimands under interference. We study the minimax risks of three problems: optimizing an estimator under a fixed design, optimizing an estimator and a design from a restricted class, or optimizing both over all designs. In each problem, we study the minimax risk over all estimators and over design-unbiased estimators separately. We allow any correctly specified exposure mapping, including the identity. Our theory represents causal inference under interference as a constrained sampling design on an expanded finite population of $\sum_i d_i$ potential outcomes, where $d_i$ is the number of exposure levels attainable by unit $i$. We describe the constrained sampling design by a weighted sampling hypergraph: its vertices are the potential outcomes, its hyperedges are the maximal sets of potential outcomes that can be jointly sampled under feasible treatment assignments, and its weights are induced by the randomization design. We show that the minimax risks of the fixed-design, restricted-design, and all-design problems depend, respectively, on the weighted hypergraph induced by the design, the collection of weighted hypergraphs attainable within the restricted class, and the unweighted hypergraph. Our Bayes lower bounds compare potential-outcome tables that differ on jointly missing sets of potential outcomes: joint-missingness probabilities govern the lower bounds over all estimators, whereas the corresponding odds govern the lower bounds over unbiased estimators. We illustrate the theory for average treatment effects without interference, saturation designs under partial interference, total, direct, and contagion effects under neighborhood interference, and bipartite dose-response contrasts. The upper and lower bounds match in several settings.

    Doubly-robust causal inference for multi-valued treatment regimes with recurrent neural networks

    Jason Poulos · 2026-11-05 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Jason Poulos

    Keywords: stat.ME, stat.ML

    Abstract

    We propose a doubly-robust method to estimate the causal effects of multi-valued treatment regimes on patient outcomes. A multi-valued treatment regime assigns personalized treatments from a set of competing options based on a patient's history. Existing methods for optimal treatment regimes are largely limited to binary or continuous treatments. We extend the longitudinal targeted minimum loss-based estimator (LTMLE) to accommodate multi-valued treatments by modeling treatment assignments with a multinomial model. We further integrate recurrent neural networks (RNNs) into the LTMLE framework as learners for the outcome, treatment, and censoring models, and compare this approach to ensemble machine learning. Simulation studies show that LTMLE with either ensemble machine learning or RNNs is approximately unbiased, with close to nominal coverage, whereas sequential g-computation with ensemble machine learning is substantially biased under a dynamic rule. A multinomial treatment model performs similarly to a series of binomial regressions with ensemble machine learning, but yields more stable inference when the treatment model is an RNN.

    Causal Inference from Self-controlled Case Series

    Gellért Perényi, Marco Piccininni, Mats J. Stensrud · 2026-11-02 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Gellért Perényi
    Marco Piccininni
    Mats J. Stensrud

    Keywords: stat.ME

    Abstract

    The self-controlled case series (SCCS) design is widely used in studies of rare events, for example of vaccine side effects. Conceptually, the design has two main features. First, it requires only data from individuals who have experienced the event of interest. Therefore, the design is especially useful when the outcome is rare and the available data come from surveillance or clinical records, say, of hospitalized patients. Second, because comparisons are made within single individuals, time-invariant confounding between treatment and outcome is eliminated. We formulate a set of sufficient identification assumptions for estimating the causal effect of vaccination on an adverse outcome under the SCCS design, drawing on ideas from the causal inference literature. Unlike the existing SCCS literature, we do not consider a parametric model as the starting point. This allows us to establish when and why the SCCS design allows identification of causal effects, using data from cases only. However, because the sufficient identifiability assumptions are implausible in some applications, we also develop statistical tests for the existence of a treatment effect that remain valid when only a subset of the identification assumptions holds.

    High-Dimensional Off-Policy Evaluation without Sample Splitting using an Onsager Correction

    Saksham Jain, Guillaume Braun, Masaaki Imaizumi · 2026-11-05 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Saksham Jain
    Guillaume Braun
    Masaaki Imaizumi

    Keywords: stat.ML, cs.LG, math.ST

    Abstract

    Reward models used for off-policy evaluation (OPE) are commonly selected using held-out data. When sample size and context dimension grow proportionally, the difference in test risk between models fitted on the full sample and on a fixed fraction of it need not vanish. Held-out evaluation can therefore consistently estimate the test risk of the model trained on the smaller sample, but because the relative ranking of candidate reward models can change after full-data refitting, held-out selection can be suboptimal. We develop an Onsager-corrected, target-policy-weighted criterion that estimates the full-data reward model's out-of-sample risk under the target policy using only its training observations. This enables early stopping along the full-data gradient-descent trajectory without sample splitting. For neural networks with fixed depth and hidden width under Gaussian contexts, single-index rewards, context-dependent multi-index target policies, and known context-independent logging, we prove uniform consistency of our criterion over a fixed finite set of gradient-descent iterates and a near-oracle guarantee for the selected reward model. The same correction yields a doubly robust estimator that consistently estimates the true policy value without sample splitting, even when reward-model prediction error does not vanish. Experiments show decreasing risk-estimation error as dimension grows at a fixed sample-to-dimension ratio, and improved reward-model selection relative to held-out baselines. Empirically, our correction also reduces the root mean squared error of doubly robust value estimates computed from the same data used to fit and select the reward models.

    Breaking Point: When Hidden Confounding Flips Off-Policy Evaluation

    Vraj Detroja · 2026-11-02 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Vraj Detroja

    Keywords: off-policy evaluation, unmeasured confounding, sepsis treatment, finite-sample bias, model-based estimation, breakdown point, hospital records

    Journal: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)

    Abstract

    Off-policy evaluation (OPE) is used to decide whether a treatment policy learned from hospital records deserves a prospective trial, and every standard estimator assumes that the records contain everything the clinicians acted on. This report studies what happens when they do not, on the sepsis simulator of Oberst and Sontag (2019), extended with a hidden variable whose strength is set exactly. Because the simulator is ported to an exact transition tensor, the true value of every policy, the confounding strength Γ* under the marginal sensitivity model, and the infinite-data limit of every estimator are computed rather than estimated. The strength of confounding alone does not determine whether OPE recommends a worse policy. With infinite data, the estimators prefer withholding antibiotics, which is truly worse than the clinicians' policy, from Γ* = 12 when the hidden variable also worsens outcomes directly, and at no Γ* up to 152 when it harms only untreated patients. With 10,000 episodes, weighted importance sampling and a model-based estimate make this recommendation in 84% and 70% of datasets at Γ* = 12. With 2,000 episodes or fewer, the finite-sample biases of these two estimators, which have opposite signs, are as large as the confounding bias. Sharp sensitivity bounds at the true Γ* cover the true value in 96% to 99.9% of confounded datasets with 10,000 episodes, but a breakdown-point alarm calibrated on recorded variables fires on 86% to 100% of deploy decisions, with a precision no better than the base rate of wrong decisions.

    Learning from Unreachable Rewards: Hint-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning for Generative Recommendation

    Kangning Zhang, Haotian Fang, Xukun Luo, Hao Yin et al. · 2026-11-03 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Kangning Zhang — Shanghai Jiao Tong University
    Haotian Fang — Shanghai Jiao Tong University
    Xukun Luo — Meituan (China)
    Hao Yin — Meituan (China)
    Yang Gao — Meituan (China)
    Peng Yan — Meituan (China)
    Weiwen Liu — Shanghai Jiao Tong University
    Weinan Zhang — Shanghai Jiao Tong University
    Yong Yu — Shanghai Jiao Tong University

    Keywords: generative recommendation, credit assignment, Group Relative Policy Optimization, sequential recommendation

    Journal: ACM International Conference on Information and Knowledge Management (CIKM)

    Abstract

    Semantic-ID generative recommenders represent each item as a short sequence of discrete semantic tokens and predict the next item by autoregressively generating this token sequence. This paradigm enables a unified generation interface for item IDs, histories, and item text, but it also creates a structured optimization bottleneck during reward-based post-training: when an early semantic token enters the wrong branch of the item-token space, finite rollout groups rarely reach the ground-truth item, so group-relative optimization receives identical zero rewards and produces no useful advantage. We propose Hint-Conditioned Generative Recommendation (\method), a semantic-ID generative recommendation framework that recovers learning signal for such hard training instances. HCGRec diagnoses each instance with checkpoint rollouts and supplies a minimal target-prefix hint only when the current generator cannot reach the correct item. The model then generates the unhinted suffix under the hinted semantic branch, turning zero-reward groups into informative comparisons over item-token completions. Hinting also changes token identity: hinted prefix tokens are oracle-provided item context, while unhinted suffix tokens are sampled generation actions. We therefore introduce hint-aware credit decomposition, using supervised learning to preserve item-semantic and prefix-structure alignment for hinted tokens and GRPO to optimize the sampled suffix. Experiments on sequential recommendation benchmarks show that HCGRec substantially improves over supervised fine-tuning and vanilla reward-based post-training, while reducing zero-advantage training samples from over 70% to below 20%. The code is accessible at https://github.com/WncFht/GRec.

    The impact of temporal resampling on the safety of clinical reinforcement learning: A simulated trial

    Thomas Frost, Hrisheekesh Jayant Vaidya, Steve Harris · 2026-11-06 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Thomas Frost — University College London
    Hrisheekesh Jayant Vaidya — University College London
    Steve Harris — University College London Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust; University College London

    Keywords: binning, offline reinforcement learning, type 1 diabetes, continuous insulin infusion, glycemic control, in silico clinical trials, MIMIC-IV, UVA/Padova simulator, model safety

    Journal: PLOS Digital Health

    Abstract

    Reinforcement learning (RL) is a type of artificial intelligence for making optimal choices. In healthcare, researchers generally use offline RL (ORL), where models are trained and evaluated from retrospective observational data. To accommodate naturally irregular clinical records, researchers often resample the data into uniform time intervals before training (known as binning). However, binning may create counterfactual trajectories that do not represent clinical practice. We first quantified these distortions in patients receiving continuous insulin infusions in the MIMIC-IV intensive care database. Binning reversed the apparent causal order for over 70% of paired glucose measurements and insulin adjustments within the same interval, and frequently masked significant dysglycaemic events and merged distinct treatment decisions together. We then conducted an in silico clinical trial using 30 virtual patients with type 1 diabetes from the FDA-approved UVA/Padova simulator. The simulator was modified to include stochastic intervals between decisions and used to generate a training dataset for offline RL. We trained three ORL algorithms on both the unprocessed dataset and equivalent datasets resampled at 10-minute, 2-hour, and 4-hour intervals. When deployed back into the simulated environment, temporal resampling was found to reduce model performance by up to 60% relative to unprocessed data, with 4-hour binning causing all agents to perform worse than the dataset's baseline. Retrospective evaluation on resampled data actively obscured this effect, predicting 1.5-3x better returns than agents achieved in practice. We recommend that future research in this area prioritises datasets with natural clinical timings between decisions, which may be a necessary step before these models can be safely deployed into patient care.

    SPyCE: Skill-Policy Co-evolution for Multimodal Agents

    Ru Zhang, Weijie Qiu · 2026-11-05 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Ru Zhang — Zhejiang University
    Weijie Qiu — Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications

    Keywords: multimodal agents, skill library, tool use, visual reasoning, reinforcement learning, multimodal reasoning

    Journal: ACM International Conference on Multimedia (ACM MM)

    Abstract

    Multimodal agents that think with images iteratively manipulate visual evidence and invoke tools across many steps. Existing reinforcement learning methods reduce trajectories to scalar rewards, forcing the policy to discover reusable tool-use patterns from scratch on every new task; memory-based alternatives retain past experience, yet they rely on test-time retrieval, without updating the policy to absorb reusable patterns from that experience. Our key insight is that multimodal reasoning trajectories should be distilled into reusable skills that co-evolve with the policy during training, rather than being consumed as rewards or retrieved from a static store. To this end, we propose SPyCE (Skill-Policy Co-evolution), a framework that distills trajectories into a hierarchical skill library and updates it throughout reinforcement learning. Execution skills capture local visual operations, while workflow skills encode high-level priors that orchestrate tool use. During training, the policy model conditions on retrieved skills to guide its rollouts, while the skill library evolves using valuable rollouts generated by the policy. This creates a closed loop in which improved policies yield better skills, and the evolving skill library, in turn, provides stronger priors for policy rollouts. Experiments across eight benchmarks demonstrate that SPyCE consistently outperforms both RL-based and memory-based baselines. Further analysis reveals that both the hierarchical skill design and the co-evolution mechanism are critical to our design. These results suggest joint skill-policy optimization as a promising paradigm for building capable multimodal agents.

    R 2 -Searcher: Calibrating Retrieval and Reasoning Boundaries for Agentic Search

    Sheng Zhang, Junyi Li, Wenlin Zhang, Xiaowei Qian et al. · 2026-11-03 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:

    Junyi Li — City University of Hong Kong
    Wenlin Zhang — City University of Hong Kong

    Keywords: agentic search, multi-hop question answering, search agents

    Journal: ACM International Conference on Information and Knowledge Management (CIKM)

    Abstract

    Recent search agents for multi-hop reasoning often fail by either retrieving incomplete evidence or reasoning over irrelevant portions of the retrieved content, leading to a retrieval-reasoning boundary shift. We propose R 2 -Searcher, a novel framework that explicitly explores and calibrates the retrieval and reasoning boundaries via fine-grained, query-token-guided evidence modeling and post-retrieval reflection. Specifically, R 2 -Searcher: (1) constructs fine-grained reasoning contexts by extracting precise facts from retrieved content based on query token semantics (e.g., subjects, actions, temporal markers, and degree modifiers), thereby guiding the attention of search agent; (2) introduces a retrieval reflection mechanism that evaluates and corrects boundary deviations after each retrieval step, guiding the generation of improved queries grounded in the extracted reasoning contexts; and (3) employs an end-to-end reasoning-reflection-guided reinforcement learning algorithm, R 2 PO, which jointly optimizes both boundaries through a tree-based exploration of reasoning regions and reflections. Our method significantly enhances the quality of both retrieval and reasoning, establishing an iterative loop where retrieval and reasoning mutually enhance each other.     Giorgia Ramponi

    Keywords: cs.MA

    Abstract

    We study cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problems in which agents evolve under decoupled noisy dynamics but share a population objective. A canonical example is a mobility operator that orchestrates a fleet of vehicles to meet customer demand. The stochastic and time-varying nature of these problems calls for learning approaches, yet existing MARL algorithms do not scale: learning assignment and routing jointly over the full state-action space becomes prohibitive as the number of agents grows. Formulating the problem over the space of probability measures, we prove a separation principle: the population cost-to-go function is upper bounded by an optimal transport problem whose transportation cost is the target-conditioned single-agent cost-to-go function. In a vehicle-delivery experiment on the southern Manhattan road network, where MARL baselines are computationally prohibitive, SALT improves travel time by 15-21% over a shortest-path routing heuristic. In stochastic grid worlds, SALT reduces average travel times by 23-61% relative to MARL baselines and scales to population sizes where these methods fail.
