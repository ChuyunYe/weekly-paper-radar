    Proximal Causal Inference for Time Series from N-of-1Mobile Device Data

    Fanyu Cui, Charlotte Fowler, Xiaoxuan Cai, Jukka-Pekka Onnela et al. · 2026-10-15 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Fanyu Cui
    Charlotte Fowler
    Xiaoxuan Cai
    Jukka-Pekka Onnela
    Justin Baker
    Linda Valeri

    Keywords: stat.ME

    Abstract

    Unmeasured confounding challenges causal inference in intensive longitudinal studies, potentially biasing treatment effect estimates. The proximal causal inference (PCI) framework offers a promising approach to identification using proxies or negative control variables in the presence of hidden confounding bias. While prior literature considers the joint effect of time-varying treatments, our work extends the framework to a time series setting to estimate the contemporaneous or lagged effect of time-varying treatments. We demonstrate that under traditional PCI assumptions, we can recover unbiased effect estimation in the presence of unmeasured confounding by leveraging the intensive longitudinal nature of time series data. Specifically, we develop identifiability conditions and show that past and future observations can be employed as natural proxies for unmeasured confounders. We further develop the bridge function required for valid PCI estimation along with asymptotic variance. Simulation studies illustrate our method's validity and robustness to violation of certain PCI identification assumptions. We demonstrate the potential for studying socio-environmental exposure health effects by applying our method to a smartphone study of bipolar patients. Our work contributes to the growing literature on PCI and provides a powerful tool for analyzing longitudinal data, including in observational mobile health research prone to confounding bias.

    Graph Dirichlet Energy for Fitting-Free Bivariate Causal Inference

    Povilas Daniušis · 2026-10-15 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Povilas Daniušis

    Keywords: cs.LG

    Abstract

    We propose the Graph Dirichlet Energy Causal Inference (GDECI) criterion for distinguishing cause from effect in bivariate settings. For a candidate direction $X\to Y$, we construct a kernel similarity graph from $X$ and treat $Y$ as a graph signal. The score is the Dirichlet energy of this signal, induced by the unnormalized graph Laplacian. The reverse score swaps the variables, and the lower-energy direction is inferred as causal. The method requires neither regression fitting nor independence testing. After the empirical probability integral transform, its decisions are invariant under strictly monotone transformations of either variable, and the graph operations reduce to convolutions, allowing exact evaluation in $O(n\log n)$ time and $O(n)$ memory. At the population level, GDECI compares degree-weighted Bayes squared prediction errors plus a non-negative smoothness term of the regression function. As the bandwidth tends to zero, the weights become uniform and the smoothness term vanishes, reducing the criterion to a RECI-type comparison of expected conditional variances in copula space. Empirically, we compare GDECI with QCCD, GRCI, CAM, IGCI (two variants), RESIT, LOCI, QPE-k, and RECI (two variants), in accuracy on 1899 cause--effect pairs and in inference speed. GDECI ranked fifth of eleven methods in macro-averaged accuracy on 12 synthetic suites ($0.776$). On the real-world Tuebingen pairs, it ranked third in unweighted accuracy ($0.684$) and fifth in weighted accuracy ($0.678$). It was also the fastest of the compared methods up to $n\approx10^{4}$. The accompanying code repository: https://github.com/povidanius/gdeci.

    Flexible Doubly Robust Proximal Causal Inference for Censored Outcomes

    Dhanajit Brahma, Ruolin Meng, Lawrence Carin, Ricardo Henao · 2026-10-14 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Dhanajit Brahma
    Ruolin Meng
    Lawrence Carin
    Ricardo Henao

    Keywords: stat.ML, cs.LG, stat.ME

    Abstract

    Estimating causal effects on right-censored time-to-event outcomes is challenging under unmeasured confounding. Proximal causal inference addresses unmeasured confounding through proxy variables, but existing proximal survival methods rely largely on restrictive parametric bridge models. We introduce Proximal Survival Bridge learning (PSB), a flexible doubly robust proximal framework for censored outcomes. Building on existing proximal identification of treatment-specific survival curves, the framework yields outcome-bridge (OB), treatment-bridge (TB), and doubly robust (DR) estimators, with two flexible bridge learners: PSB-K, a kernel estimator with a low-rank time decomposition, and PSB-N, a neural estimator trained by a projected maximum-moment-restriction loss. Unlike existing flexible proximal learners for scalar outcomes, PSB directly handles censoring and survival-curve targets. On a standard SEM benchmark, a proximal semi-synthetic ACTG study, and the real-world Framingham study, PSB-K performs strongest on SEM and ACTG, whereas PSB-N is the most stable on Framingham. Additional results with modern causal survival baselines, weakened or invalid proxies, and heavier censoring assess robustness across challenging settings.

    Generalized Odds for Ordinal Outcomes: A Causal Inference Framework Adjusting for Covariates

    Runnian Huang, Dandan Shi, Lan Liu, Yunjian Yin · 2026-10-14 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Runnian Huang
    Dandan Shi
    Lan Liu
    Yunjian Yin

    Keywords: stat.ME

    Abstract

    Background: Generalized Odds (Gen Odds) provide summaries for ordinal outcomes without requiring the proportional odds assumption. However, conventional Gen Odds compares independently drawn treatment and control outcomes and targets a population-level contrast rather than an individual-level contrast. We developed an individual-level causal formulation of Gen Odds and examined its identification. Methods: We defined the Causal Gen Odds using the joint distribution of potential outcomes and identified it under conditional independence of the potential outcomes given baseline covariates. We developed a model-based estimator using nonproportional cumulative logistic regression and introduced an odds-ratio sensitivity parameter to assess departures from conditional independence. We evaluated the estimators through simulations and illustrated them using data from the ACCORD Study. Results: In simulations, the conditional-independence estimator was approximately unbiased with 95.5% confidence interval coverage under conditional independence, but bias and undercoverage increased with residual dependence. When the sensitivity-analysis estimator incorporated the data-generating dependence parameter, bias remained below 0.01 and coverage ranged from 93.7% to 96.1% across examined settings. In the ACCORD Study, the Causal Gen Odds for right-foot pre-tibial edema was 0.909 (95% CI: 0.880 to 0.940) under conditional independence and remained stable across the examined sensitivity range. Conclusions: The Causal Gen Odds provides an individual-level estimand that complements the conventional population-level Gen Odds. This framework enables estimation of this contrast while explicitly assessing its robustness to assumptions about dependence between potential outcomes.

    Urban green space as a public health intervention: a systematic review of causal inference methods in observational studies

    Jun Ying, Hao Chen (5190), Yiqi Zhang, FENG Ying et al. · 2026-10-14 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Jun Ying — Zhejiang A & F University
    Hao Chen (5190) — Zhejiang A & F University
    Yiqi Zhang — Zhejiang A & F University
    FENG Ying — Zhejiang A & F University
    Hui Yang — Zhejiang A & F University

    Keywords: urban green space, public health interventions, causal inference, observational studies, causal effect estimation

    Journal: Humanities and Social Sciences Communications

    Abstract

    In the context of rapid urbanization, urban green spaces (UGS) are widely promoted as a lever for improving population health, yet a central debate remains: are reported health benefits an urban myth or a substantiated causal reality? We systematically review recent empirical studies that apply causal inference designs to estimate the health effects of UGS, including matching, synthetic control method (SCM), instrumental variable (IV), difference-in-difference (DID), and regression discontinuity design (RDD). We identified 37 records, of which 33 were empirical studies (excluding 4 Research protocols). Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I, and we scrutinized whether key identification assumptions were explicitly addressed for each design. Across the 33 empirical studies, 30 report a positive causal effect of UGS on at least one health outcome. Crucially, this positive consensus remains consistent among the most methodologically rigorous papers, such as those meeting all key DID assumptions or effectively combining PSM and DID. Rather than merely echoing the limitations of data and design, this review underscores that when endogeneity and self-selection bias are strictly controlled, the health-promoting role of UGS remains highly credible. Future studies should integrate multiple approaches, leverage big data and machine learning technologies, and foster interdisciplinary collaborations to enhance causal inference accuracy and provide scientific guidance for UGS planning and public health policy.

    Causal inference in conservation policy: Evaluating the effects of Protected Areas on deforestation and well-being in the Peruvian Amazon

    Teresa L. Paredes Quiroz, Karen I. Eckhardt · 2026-10-14 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Teresa L. Paredes Quiroz — Universidad Antonio Ruiz de Montoya
    Karen I. Eckhardt — Universidad Antonio Ruiz de Montoya

    Keywords: protected areas, deforestation, household welfare, conservation policy, Peruvian Amazon, regression discontinuity design, staggered difference-in-differences

    Journal: PLoS ONE

    Abstract

    The Peruvian Amazon, one of the most biodiverse regions on Earth, is under increasing pressure from deforestation, threatening both ecological wealth and local livelihoods. Protected Areas (PAs) have emerged as one of the main conservation strategies; however, questions remain regarding their effectiveness in reducing deforestation and their socioeconomic impact. This study evaluates the effects of PAs, their Buffer Zones (BZs), and Regional Conservation Areas (RCAs) on deforestation and household welfare between 2001 and 2023. Using causal inference methods, geographic Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) and staggered Difference-in-Differences (DiD), the analysis integrates high-resolution spatial data on forest loss with georeferenced household information. Results indicate that PAs significantly reduce long-term deforestation by 1.3 to 2.8 ha/km2, while BZs mitigate forest loss by 1.1 to 1.5 ha/km2. However, no significant deforestation effects are observed in the short term. Regarding welfare, households within BZs exhibit lower per capita income and expenditure, particularly near indirect-use PAs. The study also provides exploratory evidence on RCAs, a category rarely examined in the literature. Although their estimated effects are smaller and not statistically significant, their inclusion highlights the growing role of regional governance in conservation policy. These findings indicate that conservation outcomes strengthen over time and depend on local governance, institutional capacity, and the ability to contain pressures from extractive activities. This research contributes new empirical evidence to inform conservation policy design, emphasizing the need to integrate ecological and social dimensions into Protected Area management.

    A Causal Inference Protocol for Root-cause Diagnosis in Non-stationary Industrial Time Series

    Cansu Yalim · 2026-10-12 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Cansu Yalim — Old Dominion University

    Keywords: root cause diagnosis, industrial fault diagnosis, non-stationary time series, regime detection, causal inference, intervention effect estimation, Markov equivalence classes, identifiability, Tennessee Eastman process

    Journal: ODU Digital Commons (Old Dominion University)

    Abstract

    Industrial fault diagnosis increasingly relies on data-driven methods, yet most approaches assume process stationarity and treat association-based rankings as sufficient evidence for root-cause identification. Neither assumption holds when operating phases and fault progression create distinct behavioral regimes with shifting relationships among variables, and neither answers which feasible corrective action would alter a fault trajectory. The absence of a procedure addressing non-stationarity, causal identifiability, and decision-relevant effect estimation constitutes the gap this dissertation targets. In this context, a regime is a time period in which the process operates under a relatively consistent setting, so the relationships among variables are approximately stable within that period. This research develops a three-stage, regime-aware causal diagnostic protocol grounded in a Time-Varying Dynamic Bayesian Network (TV-DBN). Stage I detects regime boundaries and learns regime-specific causal skeletons via MDL optimization with Gaussian Process node models. Stage II constructs a Markov equivalence class representation using kernel conditional independence tests and Meek's orientation rules, preserving directional ambiguity. Stage III estimates do-operator intervention effects within MDL-weighted DAG extensions, gated by identifiability and positivity checks. When assumptions are violated, the protocol returns explicit non-estimability outputs, converting validity failures into auditable records. The protocol is designed and evaluated using Design Science Research Methodology, with stages traceable to the stated requirements. Evaluation uses the Tennessee Eastman Process benchmark under two fault scenarios: Fault 6 represents an actuator-level feed loss with a directed causal path to the monitored output, while Fault 1 represents a compositional disturbance in which no actuator achieves directed reachability to the target variable (fewer than 5% of sampled DAG extensions contain a directed path in any regime). Regime-aware segmentation, equivalence-class learning, and identifiability-gated effect estimation produce outputs calibrated to what observational data can defensibly support. For Fault 6, the protocol identifies xmv(3) as the sole structurally supported actuator-level candidate with robust directed reachability across all regimes, while signaling that the quantitative verification condition is not met: the estimated effect is directionally consistent but statistically inconclusive at the 95% level, satisfying the structural but not the quantitative condition. For Fault 1, the protocol withholds Regime 2 and 3 estimates due to ancestral support gate failures and refrains from promoting any actuator to root-cause status. The protocol advances practice by replacing association-driven rankings with intervention-grounded assessments and converting validity failures into actionable audit records.

    The Challenges of Hyperparameter Tuning for Accurate Causal Effect Estimation

    Damian Machlanski, Spyridon Samothrakis, Paul Clarke · 2026-10-16 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Damian Machlanski — University of Edinburgh
    Spyridon Samothrakis — University of Essex
    Paul Clarke — University of Essex; Institute for Social and Economic Research

    Keywords: hyperparameter optimization, causal effect estimation, model selection, evaluation metrics, observational data, base learners, benchmark dataset

    Journal: Machine Learning

    Abstract

    Abstract ML is playing an increasingly crucial role in estimating causal effects of treatments on outcomes from observational data. Many ML methods (‘causal estimators’) have been proposed for this task. All of these methods, as with any ML approach, require extensive hyperparameter tuning. For non-causal predictive tasks, there is a consensus on the choice of tuning metrics (e.g. mean squared error), making it simple to compare models. However, for causal inference tasks, such a consensus is yet to be reached, making a any comparison of causal models difficult. On top of that, there is no ideal metric on which to tune causal estimators, so one must rely on proxies. Furthermore, the fact that model selection in causal inference involves multiple components (causal estimator, ML regressor, hyperparameters, metric), complicates the issue even further. In order to evaluate the importance of each component, we perform an extensive empirical study on their combination. Our experimental setup involves many commonly used causal estimators, regressors (‘base learners’ henceforth) and metrics applied to four well-known causal inference benchmark datasets. Our results show that hyperparameter tuning increased the probability of reaching state-of-the-art performance in average ( $$65\% {\rightarrow } 81\%$$ ) and individualised ( $$50\% {\rightarrow } 57\%$$ ) effect estimation with only commonly used estimators. We also show that the performance of standard metrics can be inconsistent across different scenarios. Our findings highlight the need for further research to establish whether metrics uniformly capable of state-of-the-art performance in causal model evaluation can be found.

    Reinforcement Learning for Preventable Hospitalization and Readmission Reduction: A Structured Narrative Review

    Jun Pan · 2026-10-17 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Jun Pan

    Keywords: Not provided by source

    Journal: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)

    Abstract

    Purpose: To critically assess reinforcement learning (RL) for sequential interventions designed to reduce preventable hospitalizations and readmissions, distinguishing computational feasibility from demonstrated clinical effectiveness. Methods: A structured narrative review synthesized direct utilization-focused policies, adjacent inpatient decision policies, and contextual prediction research through 9 October 2026. Outcomes, Markov decision formulations, off-policy estimators, and methodological limitations were analyzed without meta-analysis. Results: Directly relevant peer-reviewed evidence centers on Medicaid care management and post-discharge intervention planning. A retrospective state-action-reward-state-action (SARSA) study estimated a 12-percentage-point reduction in an emergency visit and hospitalization composite; however, this reflects a model-based counterfactual estimate rather than an observed randomized treatment effect. A larger offline study reported non-inferiority on a shaped reward scale alongside narrower intervention-allocation disparities. Data-pooling research contributed theoretical regret bounds and empirical support for learning across patient classes. Critical limitations across the literature include unmeasured confounding, limited action support, surrogate rewards, overlapping cohorts, and absent external validation. No direct study established a prospective randomized reduction in hospitalizations from an executed RL policy. Conclusion: RL provides a coherent framework for longitudinal care allocation, but current evidence supports algorithmic development rather than proven clinical efficacy. Translation demands explicit endpoint definitions, rigorous off-policy evaluation, prospective silent workflow evaluation, and controlled trials with safety, equity, and resource constraints.

    Conformal Policy Selection: When to Deploy AI Decisions for Targeted Intervention

    Junyu Cao, Ruijiang Gao, Maytal Saar-Tsechansky, Mingzhang Yin · 2026-10-16 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Junyu Cao
    Ruijiang Gao
    Maytal Saar-Tsechansky
    Mingzhang Yin

    Keywords: stat.ME, cs.LG, stat.ML

    Abstract

    Artificial-intelligence (AI) policies are increasingly used to make consequential decisions in medicine, finance, education, and public services, yet deploying them responsibly requires knowing not only whether a policy helps on average, but for whom. Existing tools for off-policy evaluation and safe policy learning provide population-level guarantees, but some individuals may experience adverse treatment effects. We recast targeted deployment as a multiple-testing problem: for each candidate unit, test whether the outcome under the AI policy would exceed a user-specified threshold, and control the false discovery rate (FDR) among the selected set. Our method, Conformal Policy Selection, combines weighted conformal $p$-values that account for the distributional shift from behavior to target policy with a two-step selection-and-pruning procedure. We prove finite-sample FDR control and establish a doubly robust asymptotic guarantee, where FDR remains controlled if either the outcome model or the propensity model is correctly specified. Synthetic experiments verify these guarantees across four misspecification regimes, and a semi-synthetic study built on real electronic-health-record covariates illustrates how the same FDR control can act as a deployment safety gate, bounding the harmful treatments that a globally trained policy would otherwise administer to a high-risk subpopulation where it misgeneralizes. The framework offers a principled method for safe AI deployment in high-stakes applications.

    Generative Policy Improvement from Observational Data

    Jonas Schweisthal, Dennis Frauen, Stefan Feuerriegel, Emma Brunskill · 2026-10-16 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Jonas Schweisthal
    Dennis Frauen
    Stefan Feuerriegel
    Emma Brunskill

    Keywords: cs.AI, cs.LG

    Abstract

    We study how to improve decision policies from observational data to generate better actions when actions are complex, structured objects, such as text messages or images. This task is subject to two major challenges: (i) action-level overlap is often too weak for reliable off-policy evaluation and improvement, and (ii) generating new structured actions often requires expensive training or fine-tuning (e.g., of an LLM) and can be ill-defined due to information loss in action representations. To address both, we propose PolicyLift, a three-stage framework for generative policy improvement with structured actions from observational data. In Stage 1, PolicyLift learns a small set of discrete action codes that capture high-level strategies in the observed action space and are tailored to support policy improvement under limited overlap. In Stage 2, it learns a context-dependent policy over these action codes. At deployment time, Stage 3 "lifts" the learned code-level policy back to the original action space by generating actions from the historical behavior system in a code-conditioned way. We show theoretically that improving the policy over action codes guarantees improvement of the induced policy in the original action space. We demonstrate PolicyLift across experiments in synthetic and real-world text generation tasks with consistent policy improvements over the behavior policy.

    Compact Reinforcement Learning: Resource-Efficient Formulations for Large-Scale Decision Making

    Shreyas Chaudhari · 2026-10-16 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Shreyas Chaudhari

    Keywords: off-policy evaluation, distributional reinforcement learning, state abstraction, model-based reinforcement learning, large state spaces, long-horizon decision-making

    Journal: ScholarWorks@UMassAmherst (University of Massachusetts Amherst)

    Abstract

    Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a general framework for addressing problems involving sequential decision-making. However, modern practical applications of RL involve prohibitively large state and action sets: such as those in slate recommendations, image-based control, and large language-modeling tasks. Standard formulations of these problems demand intractable amounts of data and computational resources. Achieving high performance without incurring high resource costs requires compact problem formulations that capture task-relevant information while abstracting away the rest. This thesis develops and analyzes such compact formulations for decision-making problems characterized by large action and large state sets, as well as the corresponding challenge of obtaining reward feedback over them. We first address the challenge of large action sets encountered in off-policy evaluation (OPE) for slate recommendations. Risk-sensitive metrics (such as quantiles and CVaR), common for evaluating performance in this setting, require estimation of the full performance distribution from logged data rather than just the expected performance. This requires distributional OPE, which in this high-dimensional setting is highly sample-inefficient and suffers from high variance. We introduce a low-variance variant of the standard estimator that approximates the joint influence of all action dimensions through a simpler combination of marginal effects, and establish conditions under which the resulting estimator remains unbiased. Empirical evaluations on real-world datasets demonstrate that even when these conditions are not strictly satisfied, the proposed method outperforms more general baselines. We next focus on large state spaces and long-horizon problems, where classical OPE methods become intractable or error-prone. We develop a framework that combines state abstraction with a model-learning approach that distills complex, potentially continuous problems into compact, discrete models called abstract reward processes (ARPs), which preserve sufficient information about policy performance. Within the proposed OPE framework, these ARPs are estimated from off-policy data and we prove that their predictions are consistent. Varying the granularity of the state abstraction function reveals a broad range of previously unexplored hybrid ARP-based OPE estimators, encompassing classical methods as the limiting cases. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that estimators within this framework outperform existing OPE baselines across a variety of domains. Finally, we study the problem of reward selection under limited feedback constraints in reinforcement learning. While large amounts of state transition data can often be collected or generated cheaply in modern applications, obtaining reward feedback is practically expensive, particularly when relying on human feedback, over large state and action sets. In such cases, when RL is constrained to operating with limited or partial reward feedback, a central question arises: which subset of data should be reward-labeled to maximize policy performance? We formalize this reward selection problem and empirically investigate several sampling-based selection strategies, aiming to identify selection patterns that generalize across domains and enable more efficient use of reward feedback. Our results show that effective selection strategies can achieve performance comparable to learning from a fully labeled dataset while reward-labeling only a small fraction of the data, enabling feedback-efficient reinforcement learning in large-scale resource-constrained problems.

    RoboFQE: Rollout-Free Evaluation of Visual Robot Policies from Offline Trajectories

    Yunfu Deng, Sangwoo Shin, Brahma S. Pavse, Josiah P. Hanna · 2026-10-13 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Yunfu Deng
    Sangwoo Shin
    Brahma S. Pavse
    Josiah P. Hanna

    Keywords: cs.RO

    Abstract

    Evaluating robot policies before deployment typically requires producing new trajectories through real-world execution, simulators, or learned world models. These rollout-based approaches impose infrastructure requirements; simulator and world-model evaluators additionally face prediction errors that can compound over the evaluation horizon. In this paper, we study off-policy evaluation as an alternative to rollout-based robot-policy evaluation methods. Specifically, we introduce RoboFQE, an evaluator that estimates the discounted expected return of a target policy from offline robot trajectories without estimating a behavior-policy density, combining fitted Q-evaluation with a pretrained vision foundation model and learned terminal success annotation to bridge the gap between standard OPE benchmarks and continuous-control visual manipulation. Controlled experiments across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks show that RoboFQE produces accurate discounted-return estimates and preserves policy rankings even on long-horizon manipulation tasks where video world-model evaluators are degraded by autoregressive prediction errors. Ablation experiments show that pretrained vision encoders substantially outperform from-scratch alternatives, particularly on long-horizon tasks, and that learned terminal success annotation enables evaluation without ground-truth reward labels with only slight reduction of accuracy.

    The hive mind is a single reinforcement learning agent

    Karthik Soma, Yann Bouteiller, Heiko Hamann, Giovanni Beltrame · 2026-10-17 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Karthik Soma — Mila - Quebec Artificial Intelligence Institute; Polytechnique Montréal
    Yann Bouteiller — Mila - Quebec Artificial Intelligence Institute; Polytechnique Montréal
    Heiko Hamann — University of Konstanz
    Giovanni Beltrame — Mila - Quebec Artificial Intelligence Institute; Polytechnique Montréal

    Keywords: hive mind, group decision making, imitation, reinforcement learning, multi-armed bandits, group intelligence, collective learning, online learning

    Journal: Swarm Intelligence

    Abstract

    Abstract Decision-making is an essential attribute of any intelligent agent or group. Natural systems are known to converge to effective strategies through at least two distinct mechanisms: collective decision-making via imitation of others, and trial-and-error by a single agent. This paper establishes an equivalence between these two paradigms. We show that the emergent distributed cognition (sometimes referred to as the hive mind ) arising from individuals following simple, local imitation-based rules is that of a single online reinforcement learning (RL) agent aggregating action-value samples from many parallel instances of a multi-armed bandit. More specifically, we show that, in the blindly imitative weighted voter model of honey bees’ waggle dance, the update rule through which this macro-agent learns is an RL algorithm that we coin Maynard-Cross Learning . Our analysis implies that a group of individuals following simple imitative strategies can be equivalent to a more complex learning entity, substantiating the idea that group-level intelligence may explain how seemingly irrational individual behaviors are selected in nature. Beyond biology, the framework offers new tools for analyzing economic and social systems where individuals imitate successful strategies, effectively participating in a collective learning process. Our findings may further inform the design of scalable RL-inspired collective systems in artificial domains.

    tempestpy: Easy-to-Use Shielded Reinforcement Learning

    Stefan Pranger, Bettina Könighofer · 2026-10-17 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Stefan Pranger — Graz University of Technology
    Bettina Könighofer — Graz University of Technology

    Keywords: Not provided by source

    Journal: Lecture notes in computer science

    Abstract

    Abstract Safe exploration is a key challenge in Reinforcement Learning (RL) that aims to prevent agents from making decisions that could cause harm while exploring their environment. Shielding is one such technique that assumes domain knowledge in the form of a model of the environment to decide upon the safety of an action. Although shielding is a well-established approach, it has seen limited application in RL due to significant barriers. The main obstacle is the lack of an accessible end-to-end infrastructure that connects formal shield synthesis with standard RL frameworks. As a result, applying shielding requires expertise in formal methods and substantial engineering effort to construct probabilistic models, which has kept it outside the typical RL workflow. We address this problem by extending our shield synthesis tool Tempest into a practical backend for safe RL. Our core contribution is tempestpy , a Python library that integrates Tempest -based shield synthesis directly into the Gymnasium API, allowing shields to be synthesised and deployed within existing RL pipelines. This substantially lowers the barrier to entry for shielding and turns formal safe-exploration methods into a usable component for RL practitioners. In addition, we extend Tempest ’s algorithmic support to compute sound shields for stochastic multiplayer games, thereby preserving formal safety guarantees. In this setting, specifying a model of the environment becomes the remaining requirement rather than the primary integration challenge. We demonstrate the resulting workflow end to end and evaluate shielded and unshielded RL across multiple environments. To facilitate the modeling step, we provide symbolic models for Minigrid and introduce $$\textsc {Minigrid}^{\small {\textsc {Safe}}}$$ M I N I G R I D S A F E , a collection of playground environments designed to make shielding easily accessible and experimentally transparent. $$\textsc {Minigrid}^{\small {\textsc {Safe}}}$$ M I N I G R I D S A F E extends Minigrid with safety-oriented scenarios featuring probabilistic transitions and additional agents, enabling the study of challenging safety aspects in a simple and intuitive setting.

    Reinforcement learning to disentangle multiqubit quantum states from partial observations

    Pavel Tashev, Stefan Petrov, Matthew T. Diaz, Friederike Metz et al. · 2026-10-16 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Pavel Tashev
    Stefan Petrov
    Matthew T. Diaz
    Friederike Metz
    Alaina M. Green
    Norbert M. Linke
    Marin Bukov

    Keywords: multiqubit entanglement, reduced density matrix, quantum control, NISQ devices, trapped-ion quantum computing, actor-critic reinforcement learning, Haar random states, noise robustness

    Journal: Physical Review X

    Abstract

    Using partial knowledge of a quantum state to control multiqubit entanglement is a largely unexplored paradigm in the emerging field of quantum interactive dynamics with the potential to address outstanding challenges in quantum state preparation and compression, quantum control, and quantum complexity. We present a deep reinforcement learning (RL) approach using an actor-critic algorithm for constructing short disentangling circuits for states with up to 16 qubits. With access to only two-qubit reduced density matrices, our agent decides which pairs of qubits to apply two-qubit gates on; requiring only local information makes it directly applicable on modern NISQ devices, as we demonstrated experimentally on a trapped-ion quantum computer. Utilizing a permutation-equivariant transformer architecture, the agent can autonomously identify qubit permutations within the state, and adjusts the disentangling protocol accordingly. Once trained, it provides circuits from different initial states without further optimization. We demonstrate the agent's ability to identify and exploit the entanglement structure of multi-qubit states. We analyze the disentangling circuits constructed by the agent for 4- and 5-qubit Haar-random states, and observe strong correlations between consecutive gates and among the qubits involved.
