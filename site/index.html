    Higher Order Asymptotics of Regression Adjustment Estimators for Causal Inference

    Anna Kelley, Stefan Wager · 2026-10-23 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Anna Kelley
    Stefan Wager

    Keywords: stat.ME, math.ST

    Abstract

    Regression adjustment is widely used to improve the precision of average treatment effect estimates from randomized controlled trials. Interacted regression weakly dominates simple regression in terms of its first-order behavior but, in 50-50 randomized trials, the asymptotic variance of both methods is the same and so relative performance is determined by higher-order behavior. Here, we conduct a higher order analysis to compare simple vs. interacted regression adjustments in the equal-probability case. The relative performance of the methods depends on a number of factors including feature skewness, treatment-effect heterogeneity, heteroskedasticity, and nonlinear misspecification, and can favor either estimator. However, in some practically important settings, including when features are skewed and outlying feature values are associated with higher outcome noise, simple regression can outperform interacted regression by a material amount.

    Causal inference in staggered adoption panels: The correct comparison set depends on your intervention

    Ian Lundberg, Yaling Xu · 2026-10-22 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Ian Lundberg
    Yaling Xu

    Keywords: stat.AP, econ.EM

    Abstract

    A powerful data structure for causal inference is staggered adoption: many units are observed over many time periods, during which some units adopt an irreversible treatment at various time points while others remain untreated. Popular methods that apply in this setting include difference in difference, fixed effects, matching, and synthetic control. All of these methods compare the future outcomes of treatment adopters and non-adopters to answer the question: for units who adopt treatment, what outcomes would have been realized if they had remained untreated? We show that this question actually hides two very different causal questions of interest. The first is what would have happened under a momentary intervention to prevent treatment at its factual adoption time. The second is what would have happened to a treated unit under a longitudinal ban preventing treatment at all periods until outcome measurement. We show how to answer each question under conditionally ignorable treatment adoption or under parallel trends. We highlight that common methods cannot answer the second question because this question requires a time-varying comparison set with time-varying weights.

    Causal inference in oral health research: Beyond statistical significance toward a causal framework

    Jack M. Wolf, Nandita Mitra, Michael Glick · 2026-10-22 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Jack M. Wolf — University of Pennsylvania
    Nandita Mitra — University of Pennsylvania
    Michael Glick

    Keywords: causal inference, oral health research, causal reasoning, estimands, counterfactual reasoning, directed acyclic graphs, identification assumptions, abductive reasoning, periodontal treatment, cardiovascular disease, confounding, mediation, particle colliders, ICH E9(R1), research design, causal effect estimation, causal framework, structural uncertainty

    Journal: Journal of Periodontology

    Abstract

    BACKGROUND: Oral health research has adopted sophisticated statistical methods, yet studies routinely present adjusted associations as causal effects. The problem is not statistical competence but the absence of explicit causal reasoning. This commentary proposes a principled framework for causal reasoning in oral health research. METHODS: We conducted a narrative synthesis of foundational and recent literature on causal inference, drawing on texts describing the potential outcomes and counterfactual frameworks, the ICH E9(R1) regulatory addendum on estimands, study design, directed acyclic graphs, and recent applications of causal methods in oral health research; recurring principles were organized into a sequential four-pillar framework, illustrated throughout with a worked example examining periodontal treatment and cardiovascular disease. RESULTS: First, the question is framed through abductive reasoning and translated into a precisely defined estimand. Second, study design is aligned with that estimand by specifying the population, exposure contrast, and time origin so that the data reflect the intended causal comparison. Third, counterfactual reasoning and directed acyclic graphs clarify the conditions under which causal conclusions are warranted, guiding principled adjustment. Fourth, estimation and inference are used to evaluate evidence for the estimand, and results are interpreted with explicit attention to the assumptions required for causal claims CONCLUSION: We propose a cultural shift in oral health research where causal effects are precisely defined, identification assumptions are explicitly defended, and structural uncertainties are acknowledged. This framework reveals that statistical sophistication cannot substitute for careful causal reasoning. Advanced methods such as multivariable regression, propensity scores, and machine learning can allow robust estimation of causal effects but do not replace the need for a prior causal reasoning and identification assumptions. The question is not whether analyses are statistically correct, but whether causal effects are well-defined and assumptions are explicit. Applying this framework would make causal assumptions transparent, acknowledge uncertainty inherent in observational research, and clarify when experimental designs become necessary. PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY: This commentary argues that oral health research has a fundamental problem: studies use sophisticated statistics but still present associations as if they were causal findings. Better math alone doesn't fix flawed reasoning. The authors propose a four-pillar framework to address this. First, researchers should use abductive reasoning to frame a precise causal question that specifies the exposure, population, outcome, and comparison of interest. Second, study design should be aligned to that question. Third, causal assumptions must be made explicit using counterfactual thinking and directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), which clarify confounders, mediators, and colliders. Finally, statistical estimation and inference should come last, quantifying uncertainty about a pre-specified causal quantity, not generating causal meaning on their own. The bottom line: no statistical method, however advanced, can substitute for explicit causal reasoning. The real question in oral health research is not whether the analysis is statistically correct, but whether the causal effect is clearly defined and the underlying assumptions are honestly defended.

    Artificial Intelligence Causal Inference of Community Clinic Referral Transport Voucher Allocation Under Unmeasured Road-Impassability Confounding by Seasonal Flooding: A Healthcare Instrumental Variable Equity Evaluation

    Jean-Paul Morel, Danielle Adeline Hoareau · 2026-10-21 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Jean-Paul Morel
    Danielle Adeline Hoareau

    Keywords: community clinics, seasonal flooding, unmeasured confounding, health care equity, local average treatment effect, power analysis, minimum detectable effect, causal inference

    Journal: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)

    Abstract

    Community clinic referral transport voucher programmes allocate scarce resources to patients who must reach higher-level care, but seasonal flooding makes road impassability an unmeasured confounder that biases naive equity comparisons. The design parameters were clusters of 8, 16, 24 and 32, effect sizes of 3, 5 and 8 percentage points, and 2000 replications per cell, with a between-cluster standard deviation of 6.0, a residual standard deviation of 4.5 and an intraclass correlation of 0.64. The analysis is a replication-based evaluation of the before-after-control-impact estimator; no observational data are used or claimed. Power curves, minimum detectable effect curves, kernel density estimates, residual diagnostics and coefficient plots were generated from the declared variance structure. The framework proposes that the interaction instrument identifies the local average treatment effect of voucher allocation among compliers whose road access is plausibly exogenous to unobserved clinic-level demand, and the analysis is designed to identify the conditions under which the design attains 80 per cent power. The contribution is a formal identification argument and a design-characterisation protocol for equity evaluation of transport vouchers under seasonal road-impassability confounding.

    Estimand frameworks for causal inference in clinical research and regulatory decision making

    Miguel A. Hernán, Mats J. Stensrud, Xabier García-Albéniz, Issa J. Dahabreh et al. · 2026-10-20 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Miguel A. Hernán
    Mats J. Stensrud
    Xabier García-Albéniz
    Issa J. Dahabreh
    Jonathan A. C. Sterne

    Keywords: stat.ME

    Abstract

    Causal inference for clinical and regulatory decision making requires a precise definition of the causal estimand, that is, what we want to estimate. Three frameworks are commonly used to describe causal estimands: PICO and its extensions, the target trial framework, and the E9(R1) Addendum of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). Because these frameworks were developed by different scientific communities, they operate at different levels of detail, emphasize different aspects of the causal estimand, and use different terminology for closely related concepts. Here we compare the three frameworks and translate across their terminologies. PICO is a compact framework that helps jumpstart a conversation about the causal question, but it does not encode sufficient information to fully characterize a causal estimand. The target trial framework characterizes the causal estimand precisely by specifying the components of the protocol of the randomized trial that would answer the causal question: eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, assignment, outcome, start and end of follow-up, and causal contrast. E9(R1) starts from intercurrent events and lists strategies to address them, some of which describe estimators rather than estimands, or imply estimands that are not relevant to decision makers or cannot be estimated under reasonable assumptions. We argue that the target trial framework subsumes the other two, and we provide a map of the correspondences across the three frameworks.

    More Treatments More Problems: Designing the 2026 American Causal Inference Conference Data Challenge

    George Perrett, Sameer K. Deshpande, Jennifer Hill · 2026-10-19 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    George Perrett
    Sameer K. Deshpande
    Jennifer Hill

    Keywords: stat.OT

    Abstract

    This paper documents the design of the 2026 American Causal Inference Conference (ACIC) Data Challenge, which uses a large set of simulations to evaluate the performance of a wide variety of causal inference estimators chosen and implemented by challenge participants. Unlike previous ACIC Data Challenges, which tended to mimic observational studies with a single binary treatment, the 2026 Data Challenge focused on randomized control trials with five arms (one control and four active treatments) to isolate key estimation challenges that arise when simultaneously estimating multiple treatment effects. We created 9,000 distinct synthetic populations corresponding to 18 distinct settings to explore variation in performance corresponding to patterns of treatment effect heterogeneity and response surface specifications. From each population, a sample of n = 1,000 was drawn for participants to estimate individual, subgroup, sample, and population-level treatment effects and identify the optimal treatment arm at each level. We describe several strategies used to generate the data to prevent Challenge participants from exploiting structural sim across datasets and ``gaming the system.'' By detailing these simulation design considerations and data generation choices, this paper provides a framework for conducting large-scale, unconfounded synthetic benchmark studies to rigorously evaluate multi-treatment causal inference procedures.

    Artificial Intelligence Off-Policy Evaluation of Sequential Community Clinic Triage Decisions Under Delayed Referral Feedback: A Healthcare Counterfactual Estimator Validity Analysis

    Juma Ally Mbarouk · 2026-10-21 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Juma Ally Mbarouk — University of Dar es Salaam

    Keywords: off-policy evaluation, counterfactual estimation, selection bias, doubly robust estimation, importance sampling, missing not at random, sequential decision model, estimator consistency, bias decomposition, health policy evaluation

    Journal: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)

    Abstract

    Off-policy evaluation (OPE) promises to estimate the value of sequential triage policies from logged community-clinic data, but referral outcomes arrive late and selectively, so the logging policy and the target policy are confounded by the same unobserved severity that drives both the decision and the eventual label. the present analysis treats the validity of counterfactual triage estimators as a formal problem rather than an empirical one. It specifies a sequential decision model in which triage actions, delayed referral feedback and latent severity interact, and it derives the conditions under which importance-sampling, self-normalised and doubly robust estimators remain consistent when feedback is delayed and missing not at random. The central result is a bias decomposition that separates the delay-induced selection term from the variance term, and a proposition showing that doubly robust estimation removes the first-order selection bias only when the propensity model is correctly specified on the observed history. A replication-based evaluation of the ['Intercept', 'exposure', 'group', 'severity'] estimator was conducted: 600 data sets of N = 500 were drawn from a declared data-generating process (intercept 12.0, exposure 0.42, group -3.1, severity 1.35, error SD 4.2), and the study characterises the behaviour of the ordinary least squares estimator under the stated process. Across the 600 replications the estimated bias was -0.00378 for the intercept and 0.00033 for exposure, with RMSE 0.8621 and 0.015 respectively, and empirical coverage of the nominal 95% intervals was 1.0 for both parameters. The analysis clarifies which OPE estimators can be trusted for sequential triage and under what identification assumptions.

    Artificial Intelligence Off-Policy Evaluation of Community Clinic Fever Triage Under Unmeasured Antimalarial Co-Administration: A Healthcare Doubly Robust Regret Evaluation

    Efua Amankwah-Darko, Nii Tetteh Quarshie · 2026-10-21 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Efua Amankwah-Darko — University of Ghana
    Nii Tetteh Quarshie — University of Ghana

    Keywords: off-policy evaluation, community clinics, malaria, unmeasured confounding, doubly robust estimation, regret bounds, sensitivity analysis, sequential decision problems, propensity score, malaria-endemic settings

    Journal: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)

    Abstract

    Community clinic fever triage in malaria-endemic settings is a sequential decision problem in which the same patient may receive an antimalarial and a triage recommendation, and the antimalarial is recorded inconsistently across registers. Off-policy evaluation of triage policies from observational registers therefore faces unmeasured co-administration: the treatment that the policy would recommend and the treatment that was actually given are entangled. The estimator combines an inverse-propensity-weighted value with a regression-based value and is shown to be consistent when either the propensity model or the outcome model is correctly specified, but not necessarily both. A formal analysis establishes that unmeasured co-administration enters the regret as a bias term proportional to the conditional covariance between the co-administration indicator and the triage action, scaled by the inverse of the propensity score. The bias is bounded under a sensitivity parameter that quantifies the departure from sequential ignorability. The estimator is presented as a methodological proposal for register-based triage evaluation; no empirical application is reported. The analysis implies that regret comparisons between triage policies are informative only when the sensitivity parameter is reported alongside the point estimate, and that registers which record antimalarial dispensing separately from triage recommendation permit tighter bounds than registers which do not.

    Abstract

    Intraoperative blood pressure artifact, arising from non-invasive cuff cycling, vasopressor boluses and motion, corrupts the state representation on which sepsis fluid resuscitation policies are trained and evaluated.     Off-Policy Evaluation for Artificial Intelligence Agriculture Irrigation Scheduling Under Unobserved Evapotranspiration Feedback: A Computer Doubly-Robust Bound

    Sipho Mbuyiselo Ntuli, Kagiso Tumelo Mokoena · 2026-10-21 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Sipho Mbuyiselo Ntuli — University of Cape Town
    Kagiso Tumelo Mokoena — University of Cape Town

    Keywords: off-policy evaluation, irrigation scheduling, sequential ignorability, mean squared error bounds, concentration inequalities, global warming

    Journal: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)

    Abstract

    Off-policy evaluation (OPE) of irrigation scheduling policies is complicated by unobserved evapotranspiration (ET) feedback: the same soil-moisture state that a policy acts upon is also shaped by the policy's own past actions, so that the logging distribution and the target distribution differ in ways that standard importance-sampling estimators cannot correct. The bound is derived under a sequential ignorability assumption conditional on a proxy for ET, and it is shown to be sharp when the proxy is informative and to degrade gracefully when it is not. The derivation yields a finite-sample concentration inequality that accounts for the variance of the importance weights and the bias of the outcome model, and it produces a computable upper bound on the mean squared error of the OPE estimate. The bound is illustrated with a reanalysis of a public reanalysis dataset for Nairobi, Kenya (2000–2023, 312 monthly observations), in which the mean temperature is 20.146 °C and the warming trend is 0.2477 °C per decade; these values are used to parameterise the ET proxy and to show how the bound responds to a realistic warming signal. The contribution is a computer-science result: a bound that is tight enough to be useful in practice, computable from logged data alone, and explicit about the conditions under which OPE of irrigation policies can be trusted.

    Drug-resistant tuberculosis regimen selection in settings with sparse local susceptibility surveillance is a causal decision problem: the regimen that maximises expected coverage under the observed prescribing policy is not necessarily the regimen that would maximise coverage under an alternative policy, because prescribing is confounded by severity, prior treatment history and programme-level access.     Doubly Robust Distributionally Robust Offline Contextual Pricing

    Min Xu, Xinyi Yin, Yunfan Zhang, Yuxuan Han et al. · 2026-10-22 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Min Xu
    Xinyi Yin
    Yunfan Zhang
    Yuxuan Han
    Houcai Shen
    Caihua Chen

    Keywords: cs.LG

    Journal: Proceedings of the 43rd International Conference on Machine Learning, 2026

    Abstract

    Offline contextual pricing often relies on logged observational data, but faces challenges from distributional shifts between training and deployment environments. Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) provides a principled approach to off-policy evaluation and learning (OPE/L). However, existing methods are mostly limited to discrete actions. Recent work has explored DRO for continuous treatments using inverse propensity weighting (IPW), while such IPW-based estimators can be sensitive to the convergence rate of propensity score estimates, particularly when estimated nonparametrically, which may lead to larger estimation errors and regret. In this work, we develop a doubly robust (DR) framework for distributionally robust OPE/L in continuous pricing settings. For evaluation, we propose a localized DR estimator that addresses the computational challenges of worst-case expectations by fitting only a small number of regressions, comparable to standard non-robust DR, while achieving semiparametric efficiency under mild product rate conditions. For learning, we leverage the inherent smoothness of demand noise to handle pricing-specific discontinuities in revenue outcomes (e.g., threshold-based purchase decisions), establishing a finite-sample regret bound of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_p(T^{-s/(2s+1)})$ for smoothness orders $s=1,2$. This bound improves upon existing regret rates in existing DRO-based off-policy learning (OPL) for continuous treatments. Extensive experiments under various levels of distribution shift validate our proposed framework.

    Counterfactual-Policy Evaluation for Computer Agriculture Fertilizer Subsidy Targeting Under Unobserved Plot Fertility: An Artificial Intelligence Off-Policy Regret Bound

    Yassine Aït Ben Hammou, Khadija El Ouardi Berrada, Anas Bouzoubaa El Fassi · 2026-10-21 · OpenAlex

    Authors & affiliations:
    Yassine Aït Ben Hammou — Mohammed V University
    Khadija El Ouardi Berrada — Mohammed V University
    Anas Bouzoubaa El Fassi — Mohammed V University

    Keywords: fertilizer subsidies, subsidy targeting, off-policy evaluation, counterfactual policy analysis, unmeasured confounding, smallholder farming, electricity access, urbanization, GDP per capita

    Journal: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)

    Abstract

    Fertilizer subsidy programmes in smallholder agriculture allocate vouchers on observable plot and household characteristics, yet the returns to subsidized fertilizer depend on unobserved plot fertility. the present analysis treats subsidy targeting as an off-policy evaluation problem in which the logging policy is the existing allocation rule and the target policy is a counterfactual allocation rule learned from observational data. Because plot fertility is a hidden confounder, standard inverse-propensity-weighted estimators of policy value are biased, and the sign of the bias is not identified from the observed data alone.     Chenbei Lu, Zaiwei Chen, Tongxin Li, Chenye Wu et al. · 2026-10-23 · arXiv

    Authors & affiliations:
    Chenbei Lu
    Zaiwei Chen
    Tongxin Li
    Chenye Wu
    Adam Wierman

    Keywords: cs.LG

    Abstract

    Multi-step predictions of future transition realizations can improve reinforcement learning, but their value remains difficult to quantify. Directly including these predictions in the state can lead to exponential state-space growth, making direct dynamic programming computationally expensive. We study discounted finite MDPs under a block-feedback protocol: at the start of each K-period block, the decision maker observes $K$-step probabilistic transition predictions for a subset of actions and adapts decisions to observed states within the block. We show that the optimal value satisfies a Bayesian Bellman equation on the original state space. The results show that predictions can reduce transition learning to uncovered rows and that learning a near-optimal policy can require fewer prediction samples than estimating the Bayesian value function.
